Ask a guest what took long, and they rarely say the cooking. They say they waited for a menu, waited to order, then waited to get someone's attention again. Those gaps add up, and they are the easiest ones to remove.

## The three waits before the food

**Waiting for a menu.** A guest sits down and the clock starts. If the floor is busy, it can be several minutes before anyone comes over.

**Waiting to order.** Even with a menu, the guest has to catch a waiter's eye. On a full night, staff are already at other tables.

**Waiting for the order to reach the kitchen.** A handwritten ticket still has to be carried, read and sometimes asked about again.

## What table ordering changes

With a code on the table, the menu is there the moment the guest sits. They order when they are ready, not when someone is free. The order lands on the kitchen's screen straight away, with the table number and any notes, like "no pepper" or "extra egg".

Nothing gets lost in a hurry, and nobody has to remember six orders at once.

## Faster isn't rushed

Table ordering doesn't push guests out the door. It gives them control. A table that wants to linger can. A table on a lunch break can order in the first minute and be back at work on time.

Your staff still bring the food, top up drinks and welcome regulars. They just spend that time with guests instead of carrying tickets.

## Waiter calls, without the waving

Guests often need small things: water, cutlery, the bill. With a call button, they pick what they need and it goes straight to staff phones, with the table number. The guest sees when someone is on the way, so there's no waving and no wondering.

## Where to start

Start with your busiest night. Put a code on every table, and watch how much of the room's energy moves from waiting to eating.
